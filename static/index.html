"Healthy" is the range where the state's lake-level matrix rates brine shrimp, birds, wetlands, air quality from dust, and lake industries as being in good shape. Below it, exposed lakebed and rising salinity start to harm them.

      How it's worked out. USGS gauge readings and NOAA daily rain totals are pulled into a DuckDB database and added up in SQL. Gauges are found automatically: every active USGS stream gauge in the lower Bear, Weber and Jordan river basins. River water is measured at the last gauge on each river: Bear River near Corinne, Weber River near Plain City, and the Jordan at Surplus Canal and Cudahy Lane. Every other gauge drains through those, so it isn't added again. Periods up to two months use 15-minute readings averaged by hour; longer ones use USGS daily averages. Rain on the lake is the middle value of lowland stations (below 4,500 ft) spread over the lake's surface at its level then; it's skipped for periods over 20 years. Lake change compares the average of the day before the period with its last day, which smooths out some wind.

      Left out. Evaporation, groundwater, smaller creeks and drains that enter the lake directly, and canal diversions above the gauges. Recent readings are provisional and may be revised.

      Data sources

      • Stream flow and lake level: USGS National Water Information System, instantaneous and daily values. Recent values are provisional.
      • Rain: NOAA Applied Climate Information System, daily reports from NWS co-op, airport and CoCoRaHS stations.
      • Lake area and volume: USGS Great Salt Lake elevation-area-volume tables (2023).
      • Healthy range 4,198–4,205 ft: the levels where Utah's Great Salt Lake elevation matrix (Division of Forestry, Fire and State Lands, 2013) rates most of the lake's resources as healthy. The Great Salt Lake Strike Team recommended it as the target, and the 2024 Great Salt Lake Strategic Plan aims for its low end. Record low: USGS.
      • Satellite picture: Copernicus Sentinel-2 Level-2A via Microsoft Planetary Computer (zoomed in) and Sentinel-2 cloudless 2024 by EOX, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (zoomed out). Map text: MapLibre demo fonts; place names: Esri World Boundaries and Places (Esri, HERE, Garmin, OpenStreetMap contributors).
      • Unit conversions: 1 acre-ft = 325,851 gallons; an Olympic pool holds 2,500 m³ (about 660,000 gallons).
